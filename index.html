In the Good Old Days

Interviewer: Cecilia Yuan
Storyteller: My Mom
Location: Chongqing, China
Time Period: 1970s

Life Before the Internet

My mom was born and raised in Chongqing, China, in the 1970s. There was no internet, and things simply moved slowly.

Chongqing in the 1970s and today.

People often wrote letters to communicate with people far away. It usually took around ten days for a letter to arrive.

For something more urgent, there was the telegram. But even a telegram could still take around three days to arrive.

There was no way to send a message and know immediately that someone had received it. There was no way to check where someone was.

You simply had to wait.

Waiting for the News

News also spread slowly. If something happened today, the earliest my mom might read about it was the next day.

My grandparents subscribed to Chongqing Daily and Chongqing Evening News. Reading the newspaper was how they learned about what was happening outside their everyday life.

Something could already have happened somewhere far away, while they still knew nothing about it.

They would only find out later.

A World Inside Books

My mom loved reading. When she was young, she liked to read lianhuanhua, small illustrated books that told stories through a series of pictures.

An example of lianhuanhua, the illustrated books my mom read as a child.

Classic Chinese stories such as Journey to the West and Romance of the Three Kingdoms were published in this form.

Some bookstores allowed children to read without buying anything. There were also small bookshops where she could rent one for only 0.01 yuan (less than 1 cent).

When she got older, she moved on to novels. At that time, books were one of the main ways she could explore a new world beyond her surroundings.

Even new school textbooks were exciting to her. Whenever she received a new Chinese or history textbook, she would read through it immediately because everything inside felt new.

After School

Her world was closely connected to her family and friends.

After school, children spent their time together. They played games, went down to the Yangtze River to catch fish, or collected beautiful stones.

The adults had their own ways of passing the time. They played poker and mahjong, or simply sat together chatting for hours.

In Chongqing dialect, people called this “吹龙门阵.”

Then Television Came

Later, television became part of everyday life.

At first, owning a television was a big deal. Not every family could afford one.

When one family in the neighborhood bought a TV, it did not really belong only to that family.

People from the neighborhood would gather in their home to watch together.

Press ▶️ to watch the black-and-white television.

A black-and-white television and families gathering together to watch.

The television was black-and-white.
The screen was small.
There were not many channels.

But that was enough.

Everyone would sit around the television, almost as if they were sitting in a movie theater today.

There were not many TV shows either. A single television series could be shown again and again, during summer vacation and then again during winter break.

Sundays

Weekends were simple too. At that time, there was only one day off each week: Sunday.

Sometimes my mom and her family went to the zoo or a park. They brought snacks, cold noodles, and marinated meat from home, spread a plastic sheet on the ground, and spent the day outside together.

Other Sundays were for shopping.

Her family lived by the river, and the nearest department store was on the other side.

Going shopping meant taking a boat across the river.

There were not many things to choose from in the department store. New styles of clothing might only be released once a season, or even once a year.

When one style became popular, almost everyone on the street seemed to be wearing the same thing.

Sometimes they also went to wholesale markets, where there were more styles to choose from at a cheaper price.

To buy something, you had to go there

At that time, buying something simply meant going to a store and trying to find it.

There was no screen to scroll through.
No button to press.
No package arriving at the door the next morning.

If the store did not have what you wanted, you could not simply search for another one online.

You had to keep looking.

Simply Life

This was the ordinary life of my mom's childhood: waiting ten days for a letter, reading yesterday's news in the morning newspaper, renting illustrated books, and squeezing into a neighbor's home after dinner to watch the same television shows on a tiny black-and-white TV.

Today, waiting ten days for a message might make us nervous. Not knowing where someone was, whether they had received our letter, or what was happening somewhere far away might even feel frightening.

But for my mom,
it was simply life.
